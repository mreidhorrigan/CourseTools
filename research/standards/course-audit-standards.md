# Standards profile for an online higher-education course audit

This profile is an evidence-based review aid. It does not confer certification,
legal compliance, or institutional approval. Each framework retains its own
scope; an audit must not treat the frameworks as interchangeable.

## Quality Matters Higher Education Rubric, Seventh Edition (published areas)

Apply the eight publicly listed General Standards: course overview and
introduction; learning objectives; assessment and measurement; instructional
materials; learning activities and learner interaction; course technology;
learner support; and accessibility and usability. Examine constructive
alignment among objectives, assessments, materials, activities, interaction,
and technology. The proprietary detailed rubric is not supplied, and the audit
must not claim a Quality Matters review or certification.

Source: https://www.qualitymatters.org/qa-resources/rubric-standards/higher-ed-rubric

## OSCQR, current online-course quality-review areas

Apply OSCQR as a formative review across its published areas: overview and
information; technology and tools; design and layout; content and activities;
interaction; and assessment and feedback. Check orientation, transparent
expectations, predictable navigation, readable layout, meaningful interaction,
assessment directions, criteria, feedback, and links to learner support.

Source: https://oscqr.suny.edu/

## WCAG 2.2

Review authored HTML and described media against the four principles:
perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. Give course-specific
attention to text alternatives, captions or transcripts, semantic headings and
lists, meaningful link text, contrast, keyboard operation, consistent
navigation and help, labels and instructions, and accessible documents. Do not
claim conformance without applicable automated and human testing at a stated
level.

Source: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/

## CAST Universal Design for Learning Guidelines 3.0 (2024)

Look for purposeful options across engagement, representation, and action and
expression. Review learner choice and relevance; belonging and collaboration;
multiple and accessible representations; support for language, symbols, and
prior knowledge; multiple appropriate forms of expression; graduated support;
clear goals; planning; resource organization; progress monitoring; and timely,
action-oriented feedback. Identify barriers or compulsory formats that are not
required by a learning outcome.

Sources:

- https://udlguidelines.cast.org/
- https://udlguidelines.cast.org/more/downloads/

## SFU course workload and student-facing policy information

For a three-unit course, review whether the described workload communicates a
reasonable average total learning commitment and acknowledges variation among
students, weeks, and activity types. SFU's 2022 unit-definition proposal uses
the historical benchmark of about nine learning hours per week for three units
and emphasizes transparent total-workload expectations for online as well as
in-person courses. Review whether students can find applicable academic
integrity expectations and institutional support information. Treat the
institution's current policy pages as authoritative whenever local course prose
and policy conflict.

Sources:

- https://www.sfu.ca/content/dam/sfu/senate/senate-documents/2022/0404/S.22-47.pdf
- https://www.sfu.ca/students/academicintegrity/faculty/prevention/syllabus.html
- https://www.sfu.ca/students/enrolment-services/academic-integrity.html

## Audit rules

1. Use only evidence in the supplied course packet. Cite a source path and a
   heading or exact short phrase for every material finding.
2. Separate a demonstrated defect from information absent from the packet.
3. Prefer findings that affect student action, alignment, accessibility,
   assessment fairness, workload, or successful navigation.
4. Flag inconsistent, stale, ambiguous, or provisional statements.
5. Recommend the smallest practical correction and name its authoritative
   source file when possible.
6. Avoid generic praise, invented Canvas behavior, legal conclusions, and
   formal certification claims.
