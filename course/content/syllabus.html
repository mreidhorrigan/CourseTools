Course Description

IAT 210 studies the history and evolution of games from board and card games through contemporary electronic games. It examines games as rules and designed systems, games as play and experience, and games as cultural and industrial forms. Students move between reading, play, analysis, and making to ask how rules organize action, how games communicate values, how players create meaning together, and how technologies and platforms shape what designers can build.

The three project rounds introduce different forms of design practice. The first uses tabletop roleplay and actual-play recording to study facilitation, agency, consent, performance, and editing. The second uses a browser-playable procedural ecosystem to explore computational rules, interfaces, interacting entities, resources, variation, and emergence. The third uses a board game to make human-executable rules, probability, feedback, balance, onboarding, and blind testing visible. The project calendar follows a 4-5-4 sequence: four weeks of actual play, five weeks of digital game design, and four weeks of board-game design.

Delivery and weekly rhythm

The course is asynchronous, but the projects are not solo or fully self-paced. Each round uses a three-person team and requires real-time coordination for meetings, playtests, and production. Read the project instructions early and confirm that you can schedule work with classmates. Each new trio must differ from the preceding trio by at least one person.

A normal week includes one required reading, a public lecture or interview by an author, a short playable case when useful, and a practice quiz or project milestone. Readings are the principal examination material. Author media and playable cases support comprehension and application unless a module explicitly identifies additional examinable material.

Read the course rationale and design explanation for the evidence, workload model, 4-5-4 structure, assessment design, and development tools behind the course.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  • analyze games through rules, mechanics, feedback, agency, representation, labour, and platform conditions;
  • translate an experience goal into a playable and appropriately scoped prototype;
  • distinguish observation from interpretation in playtesting and use evidence to guide revision;
  • document rules, technical dependencies, permissions, licences, and accessibility decisions;
  • work in rotating teams with visible roles, action items, version history, and individual contribution evidence;
  • explain design choices through accurate use of assigned concepts and readings.

Project Design and Deliverables

Shared process for every round: establish a team goal, roles, communication expectations, a shared workspace, and named action items. Build the smallest testable version first. Test risky assumptions early. Record what players did, what failed, and what changed. Report persistent team problems before the deadline.

Actual-play podcast and tabletop roleplay

Assignment pages: Ideation | Session plan and rulebook | Final submission

  • Ideation: propose a playable situation, the participants' main activity, the intended listener experience, and one relevant course concept. Develop two classmates' ideas through specific replies.
  • Plan: submit one PDF containing the rules, playable situation, consequential choices, consent and safety process, recording workflow, role assignments, accessibility plan, dry-run question, schedule, and definition of done.
  • Final: submit one 10–15 minute MP4 gameplay episode and one ODT companion document containing a timestamped transcript, a criterion-by-criterion rubric evidence map, final rules and session materials, consent and permission records, credits, test and revision evidence, and individual contribution evidence.

The grade rewards a playable session, meaningful player agency, responsible facilitation, listener orientation, intelligibility, access, and evidence-based revision.

Digital game design: procedural ecosystem

Assignment pages: Ideation | Design and technical plan | Final submission

  • Ideation: propose a small browser-playable game with a clear player action and at least two interacting entities, resources, or environmental processes. Develop two classmates' ideas through specific replies.
  • Plan: submit one PDF containing the player loop, procedural-ecosystem state model, interaction rules, expected variation, seed or reproduction method, engine and dependency plan, browser-export plan, accessibility and testing plan, workflow, and definition of done.
  • Final: submit one ZIP with index.html at its root, all assets on local relative paths, source files, README, gameplay video, testing and revision evidence, credits and licences, and individual contribution evidence.

A small complete system is preferable to an ambitious broken one. Freeze major features 72 hours before the deadline. After the freeze, concentrate on integration, bugs, browser testing, accessibility, documentation, and export.

Board-game design

Assignment pages: Ideation | Design plan and draft rulebook | Final submission

  • Ideation: propose an audience, experience goal, core activity, play duration, and meaningful choice. Develop two classmates' ideas through specific replies.
  • Plan: submit one PDF containing the experience goal, core loop, draft rulebook, component inventory, simulations for special pieces, high-risk assumptions, playtest questions, quantitative evidence to collect, workflow, and definition of done.
  • Final: submit one ZIP containing PDF rules, PDF printable components, simulations or facsimiles for required non-printable pieces, gameplay video, playtest and balance evidence, credits, and individual contribution evidence.

Use placeholder materials before investing in presentation. At least one test should ask unfamiliar players to use the rules with little or no designer intervention. Record duration, recurring questions, dominant strategies, stalled states, score ranges, or other relevant evidence. When two competing ideas cannot be resolved by discussion, prototype and test both.

During the digital and board-game rounds, groups may use the podcast evidence method formatively: record a short MP4 playtest, prepare a labelled and timestamped ODT transcript or observation record, and map important events to requirements being tested. Separate observation from interpretation and identify the next revision or test.

AI chatlogs

AI may support brainstorming, editing, transcription, coding, debugging, and project management. It may not replace peer replies, fabricate sources, or be used during quizzes or the final examination.

When AI is used, submit the complete relevant chatlogs and nothing additional for the appendix. Paste them below an ideation post, append them to the plan PDF, or place them inside the final ZIP. No AI appendix is required when AI was not used. Remove passwords, access tokens, private third-party messages, medical information, and other confidential material before submission.

Course Policies

Communication and collaboration: Use Canvas for official course communication and submissions. Teams should agree on a response window, meeting rhythm, shared workspace, and method for recording action items. Roles can reflect strengths, but ideation, testing, feedback, and final verification remain shared responsibilities. Report sustained non-participation, safety concerns, or unresolvable scheduling problems early. Peer reports trigger an evidence review, not an automatic penalty or popularity vote. The SFU Student Conduct Policy describes expectations for respectful and responsible conduct.

Late and missing submissions: All missing submissions are recorded as 0% until they are received and graded. Late submissions receive a deduction of 0.4 percentage points from the assignment percentage for each hour late, to a maximum deduction of 100 percentage points. The late period begins at the Canvas due time and ends when a valid submission is received. Approved academic concessions supersede the ordinary late calculation.

Grade reviews and appeals: A request to review or appeal an assignment grade must be submitted within two weeks of the date the grade was released. The request should identify the rubric criterion, calculation, or submitted evidence at issue. Reconsideration may result in the grade being raised, lowered, or left unchanged. Questions about feedback are welcome, but a formal reconsideration request must arrive within the two-week window. The university process is set out in SFU’s Grading and the Reconsideration of Grades policy.

Consent, privacy, and rights: Do not record or publish classmates' voices, images, private information, or work without permission. Public release is optional and requires agreement from all contributors. Cite and follow licence terms for code, game systems, images, audio, fonts, libraries, and other assets. Do not place credentials or confidential information in repositories or submitted chatlogs. Relevant institutional requirements include SFU’s Protection of Privacy Policy and Intellectual Property Policy.

Accessibility: Accommodations are arranged through the Centre for Accessible Learning. Design teams should identify relevant access barriers early rather than adding access features only at the end. Where a playable case creates a barrier, an instructor-approved recording, transcript, or equivalent analysis route may be used. SFU’s academic concessions guidance explains the distinction between concessions and accommodations, including disability and religious accommodations.

Academic integrity: SFU academic-integrity rules apply to every stage. Submitting fabricated playtests, false contribution evidence, undisclosed copied assets, another person's work, or prohibited AI assistance is a breach of course and university expectations. Never copy and paste assignment instructions into a chatbot, document, submission, source-code file, or other location. Read the instructions in Canvas and write your own working notes in your own words. Suspected violations are addressed through SFU’s academic-integrity reporting procedures, which include notice and an opportunity for the student to respond.
