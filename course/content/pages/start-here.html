Game studies field roadmap

This roadmap locates the course's selected readings within a wider intellectual history. It is a map for orientation rather than an additional required-reading list. Game studies draws on play studies, anthropology, literary and media theory, design research, history, sociology, political economy, and critical race, gender, disability, and queer studies.

Before digital game studies: play, ritual, and classification (1938–1990s)

  • Johan Huizinga, Homo Ludens (1938), proposed play as a formative element of culture.
  • Roger Caillois, Man, Play and Games (1958), developed the influential agon, alea, mimicry, and ilinx categories and the paidia–ludus continuum.
  • Brian Sutton-Smith, The Ambiguity of Play (1997), showed that claims about play depend on competing cultural rhetorics.

Ergodic texts and field formation (1997–2004)

  • Espen Aarseth, Cybertext (1997), supplied a vocabulary for texts that require nontrivial reader activity; a copy under consideration for this course is in the instructor's reading review.
  • Janet Murray, Hamlet on the Holodeck (1997), examined digital environments as procedural, participatory, spatial, and encyclopedic media.
  • Aarseth, “Computer Game Studies, Year One” (2001), marked the launch of Game Studies and argued for games as a distinct object of study.
  • Markku Eskelinen, “The Gaming Situation” (2001), offered an early ludological account of games as configurative practice. Its sharp opposition to narrative belongs to the historically important narratology–ludology debate.
  • Gonzalo Frasca, “Simulation versus Narrative” (2003), refined the difference between representing events and modelling systems; Henry Jenkins, “Game Design as Narrative Architecture” (2004), described productive relations among games, space, and narrative.
  • Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman, Rules of Play (2003), connected formal systems, meaningful play, design, and culture. Week 5's assigned MDA essay belongs to this formal-design conversation.

Rules, fiction, procedure, and player action (2003–2012)

  • Week 1's assigned Jesper Juul essay precedes Half-Real (2005), which relates real rules to fictional worlds.
  • Ian Bogost, Persuasive Games (2007), argued that processes can make rhetorical claims; Miguel Sicart, “Against Procedurality” (2011), challenged accounts that understate players, interpretation, and context. Week 12 places these positions in conversation.
  • Sicart, “Defining Game Mechanics” (2008), and Week 9's assigned work on operational logics develop distinct accounts of how rules become playable action.

Players, communities, paratexts, labour, and industry (2006–present)

  • T. L. Taylor, Play Between Worlds (2006), and Celia Pearce, Communities of Play (2009), established ethnographic routes into players and communities.
  • Mia Consalvo, Cheating (2007), developed “gaming capital” from Bourdieu to describe game-related knowledge, competence, and status. Week 3's paratext reading and Week 11's metagame materials extend this line.
  • Julian Kücklich's “Precarious Playbour” (2005), Week 6's assigned Postigo essay, Nick Dyer-Witheford and Greig de Peuter's Games of Empire (2009), and Brendan Keogh's recent industry research connect modding and game making to labour and political economy.

History, identity, power, and exclusions (2007–present)

  • Mary Flanagan, Critical Play (2009), and Anna Anthropy, Rise of the Videogame Zinesters (2012), connect experimental design to intervention and wider access to authorship.
  • Lisa Nakamura's work on race and digital labour, Kishonna Gray's work on race and gender in online games, Adrienne Shaw's Gaming at the Edge (2014), and Bonnie Ruberg's Video Games Have Always Been Queer (2019) demonstrate that players and histories cannot be treated as culturally neutral.
  • Week 2's assigned Nooney and Trammell readings intervene in game historiography and racialized play. Weeks 7–8 carry this attention into interfaces, representation, monetization, and embodied interaction.
  • Platform studies, including Nick Montfort and Ian Bogost's Racing the Beam (2009), connects technical constraints to cultural form. Week 10 broadens game history beyond videogames through archaeological evidence and historiographic method.

The early narratology–ludology dispute helped establish questions specific to games. Subsequent scholarship has treated games simultaneously as rule systems, fictions, performances, technologies, commodities, workplaces, communities, and cultural practices.

Open-access terminology guides

Use these references to trace unfamiliar terms and then follow their citations to the originating scholarship.

  • Encyclopedia of Ludic Terms: a peer-reviewed, evolving reference for play and game research.
  • “Making Sense of Play in Video Games”: an open-access guide to ludus, paidia, and possibility spaces.
  • Barry Atkins's open-access glossary: concise definitions for videogame-analysis terms.
  • DiGRA Digital Library and Game Studies: open research archives for terms whose meanings remain contested.

Gaming capital: begin with Mia Consalvo's Cheating and use open scholarship that cites the concept when the book is unavailable. A glossary entry is a starting point; course answers should reflect the assigned author's particular use of a term.
