Course rationale and design

IAT 210 treats games as designed systems, cultural forms, industrial products, and collaborative performances. Reading, criticism, play, and production therefore appear together. The three project rounds move from facilitated tabletop roleplay and actual-play media, through computational system design, to human-executable board-game rules. This 4-5-4 sequence gives each round a distinct material and social problem while carrying observation, documentation, iteration, and critical analysis across the term.

Workload design

SFU defines one unit as approximately two to three hours of combined class and self-study time each week. A three-unit course therefore represents approximately 6–9 hours weekly, or 78–117 hours across thirteen weeks. This course uses the midpoint, 7.5 hours per week, as its planning target.

The weekly calculation uses the Wake Forest University Workload Estimator 2.0. Academic readings are entered as 600-word page equivalents with some new concepts and an “understand” reading purpose. Because the toolkit can measure words directly, it applies the equivalent rate of 130 words per minute. Video, play, quiz review, team coordination, and project production are entered as direct-duration activities. The calculation assigns remaining time after reading across those activities according to the current project round. Every module reports the resulting estimate. These estimates should be checked against student experience and revised when observed completion times differ.

Assessment design

Seven low-weight quizzes provide retrieval practice and distribute preparation across the term. Three project rounds divide work into individual ideation, a group plan, and a group final submission. The sequence makes early thinking visible, supports feedback before high-weight work, and preserves evidence of individual contribution. The cumulative in-person examination assesses students' ability to define, distinguish, apply, analyze, evaluate, and combine course concepts.

Course-development method

The course is maintained as human-editable HTML, JSONC configuration, and Testmaker source. Deterministic scripts compile Canvas-compatible inline styles, measure workload, validate links and assessment specifications, build IMSCC packages and PDFs, and make guarded Canvas API changes. AI supports research, drafting, comparison, and specification review. The public CourseTools development toolkit on GitHub documents and preserves this process.

Development and validation methodology

Course development separates human judgment, model-assisted exploration, and deterministic verification. Human-editable source files remain authoritative. Generated Canvas HTML, quiz objects, document files, reports, and course packages are treated as build products. Versioned configuration records course structure and intended settings, while scripts perform repeatable transformations and compare the resulting objects with Canvas.

Assignment and rubric boundary testing

A Mistral-based harness was used as a lower-capability boundary tester. In separate calls, the model adopted configured student profiles, produced simulated submissions from the assignment instructions without seeing the rubric, graded those simulations from the rubric, and audited ambiguities or instruction–rubric mismatches. Profiles included literal, hurried, and confidently mistaken readings. Fixed seeds and source hashes improved comparability across runs. Repeated misunderstandings prompted revisions to instructions or criteria. The rubric-calibration work also compared strong published games with deliberately incomplete simulated work to check that criteria could support both high and failing results. These simulations are diagnostic design evidence; they do not predict individual students and are never used to grade student work.

Quiz and examination preparation

A private assessment blueprint maps stable course-material identifiers to each assessment. Testable materials are stored or linked with provenance and rights information, and questions must identify the material and learning target they assess. Testmaker sources require one correct response, three plausible distractors, a Bloom level, and material traceability for each multiple-choice item. Deterministic checks examine question counts, duplicate or overlapping targets, grammar, unresolved pronouns and indexicals, incidental course-rule memorization, and coverage across Bloom's taxonomy. Printable versions use the established JavaScript renderer and its pagination checks so that a question and its response options remain together.

Canvas practice quizzes use ten two-question target groups. Canvas selects one question from each group, producing ten questions from twenty candidates while preventing two variants of the same target from appearing together. Groups are assigned across Bloom levels in a 1–2–2–2–2–1 pattern from Remember through Create, preserving the intended balance for each student. Answers are shuffled, one attempt is allowed, and each quiz is available during one specified 24-hour window. Assessment sources, answer information, copyrighted local copies, and generated forms remain in excluded private storage.

Quality assurance and deployment

Automated checks cover module order, dates, quiz settings, assignment-group weights, outcomes, rubrics, workload estimates, internal migration links, external-link behavior, uploaded-file visibility, inline-style compilation, public-package privacy, and consistency between local sources and Canvas. Whole-course copies begin with exports of both source and target courses. Live writes pass through a server restricted to one configured course and are refused when that course is published. A completed migration is followed by inventory and course-specific verification rather than being accepted solely from its completion status.

Limits and review

Model output can expose ambiguity and generate comparison material, but it cannot establish accessibility, fairness, disciplinary validity, or expected grade distributions. Link checks cannot guarantee that a human-facing page or media item remains usable. Workload estimates require comparison with student experience. The course therefore remains subject to instructor review, student feedback, accessibility review, rights review, and revision after delivery.
